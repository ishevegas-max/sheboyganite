The southside is about to become a road

If you've walked the shoreline south of King Park any time in the last forty years, you know the routine: pick your way down the bank, follow the narrow strip of sand, watch the manholes. Those concrete cylinders are the lids of a 96-year-old secret — the southside interceptor, a trunk sewer buried since 1930 that carries nearly half of Sheboygan's sewage to the plant at the foot of Lakeshore Drive.

That shoreline is about to be gone. Not eroded away. Replaced. On purpose, with federal money, by design.

FEMA approved a Hazard Mitigation Grant for the southside interceptor. About 6,000 feet of lakeshore — from the wastewater treatment plant to High Avenue — gets a stone revetment. The armor is 1-to-2-ton boulders, four feet thick. On top of that, and along the entire mile-plus, the city is building a 12-foot-wide crushed-stone access road.

Read that again. They are not just armoring the shoreline. They are building a road along the lake.

The tell is in the funding. $1.82 million in "ineligible costs" — work FEMA will not cover — includes repaving and enlarging the parking lot at the end of High Avenue. A four-way stop already went in there.

The whole project is $11.9 million. FEMA is putting in $7.8 million. The completion deadline in the paperwork is February 14, 2028.

A hazard grant is supposed to keep a sewer from failing into Lake Michigan. That part is real. A 96-year-old trunk line sitting in an eroding bank is a genuine problem. The question is what else got packed into the same job: a road, a bigger lot, a new stop, a new way to arrive at the water by car.

The July 27, 2026 Public Works packet is the source. It is public. It is a PDF. It does not say we are converting the informal southside shore into a constructed lakefront. It itemizes stone, pipe, and a parking lot FEMA itself marked ineligible.

Opinion, labeled. This project is a tourism amenity wearing a hard hat. The sewer protection is real. The parking lot is the play. Say so.

Malibu is what's ending Kite Beach

The interceptor armor stops south of King Park. It does not bury Kite Beach.

What ends the launch is Project Malibu: about 210 apartments at 1403 South 7th Street / Clara Avenue, on the grassy lot the kite and windsurf community used to park, rig, and launch.

Groundbreaking was May 2025. The money is TID 21 money — tax increment, which means the extra property tax from the new building is pledged to the district instead of the general fund.

The sand stays public by contract. That sentence will get repeated. It is true and it is not the point.

Kite Beach was never just sand. It was a free staging ground: room to park, to lay out lines, to rig a kite, to wait on wind, to teach a kid, to leave a board for twenty minutes without buying a slip or a garage. The sand without the lot is a beach you can look at. It is not a launch.

The interceptor job and Malibu are not the same project. They are in the same three-year window, on the same stretch of city, approved by the same government. One uses FEMA and the wastewater fund. The other uses a tax district. If you only read one press release, you miss the other half of the water.

Three ledgers. One appetite.

This is not one hidden plan. It is three budgets pointed at one lakefront, moving through one Public Works committee.

Ledger one: TID 21. It pays Project Malibu and marina design.

Ledger two: the Wastewater Fund plus the FEMA hazard grant. It pays the southside interceptor, the revetment, and the access road. FEMA split that job in writing. The parking lot sits in the ineligible bucket — a federal reviewer saying the lot is not required hazard mitigation.

Ledger three: Harbor Centre Marina. It sits inside a $77 million vision. Only phase one is funded.

Three names. Three funding streams. One shoreline. 2023 to 2028.

Cities do this because it works. A sewer job sounds like maintenance. An apartment building sounds like housing. A marina vision sounds like economic development. Each one has its own public hearing, its own acronym, its own friendly rendering. Nobody has to publish the sentence: we are rebuilding the lakefront.

SheVegas publishes the sentence. Follow the money across the ledgers they hope you will not add up.

1.14 miles of wild shore, gone

When the work is done, 1.14 miles of informal southside shore — the kind you walk, not the kind you enter through a lot — becomes rock and a road.

Kite Beach keeps the sand and loses the lot.

Harbor Centre Marina reconfigures docks, the admin building, and parking.

Three zones. One Public Works Committee. The window is 2023 through 2028.

Wild shore is not a park name. It is what the southside was: unarmored, un-ticketed, a place you reached on foot. A crushed-stone access road is not a trail. It is a road. It is 12 feet wide. It is being built with hazard money on top of a sewer.

The primary source is the July 27, 2026 Public Works packet. Read it yourself.

How the name happened

How SheVegas was born

Not a chamber slogan. A liquor-store nickname that stuck because the journalism did.

Every city has a nickname. Some are earned, some are imposed, and some are fought over. Sheboygan's is SheVegas — and it did not come from a marketing firm, a city hall press release, or a chamber of commerce brainstorming session.

It came from a liquor store on South 26th Street, a group of skeptical friends, and a photojournalist who decided his hometown deserved better coverage than it was getting.

Sheboygan in the mid-2000s: one newspaper, one radio station, no TV news. A tourist town on Lake Michigan with 50,000 people and zero accountability journalism. City council meetings went uncovered. Development deals happened in back rooms. The armory was rotting. The quarry was fenced off. And nobody was asking why.

The names that lost: Stupid Sheboygan People (direct, not inviting). SheBaghdad (topical for 2005, grim). Kingsburia (north side, too narrow). SheVegas was the one that stuck.

"When I launched SheVegas my buddies said it sounded like a gay porn site and suggested I call my site: 'Stupid Sheboygan People.' SheBaghdad and Kingsburia almost made the cut, but, SheVegas Baby!"

What happened next: eight mayoral candidates at the peak — the most competitive race in Sheboygan history. The Armory is still standing; it was slated for demolition. Quarry Beach opened to the public for a summer. A bloggers casino rumor dominated the most spirited election cycle the city had seen. The local newspaper was forced to start covering city government seriously.

Mid-2000s: SheVegas heard in casual use. Origin uncertain. Around 2005: SheVegas.com launched by Mike Brunette from South 26th Street. 2007–2010: peak civic impact. 2016: SheVegas is the most common nickname for Sheboygan. 2026: the same stories run on shevegas.com and shevegas.org, linked to each other.

Fun and honesty. That's the deal.

I came back to a tourist town with one newspaper, one radio station, and no TV. I launched SheVegas because local politics is where every dollar is spent and every candidate starts.

Information must be free. Original source documents and verified first-hand accounts come before anyone's spin, including mine. You can't make any money doing honest news. When you have as much information as I do, you can have great fun.
